# 2026-04 Road Trip Reflections: Oslo to Ålesund

- **Date:** April 2026
- **Context:** Solo road trip Oslo → Ålesund via Lillehammer, Gjøvik, Dombås
- **Duration:** Early April 2026 (Easter break)
- **Weather:** Very volatile — mainly cloudy, sometimes rainy, occasionally very brief sunny moments. ~5°C
- **Camera Body:** Sony A7C II (Full-Frame, E-Mount)
- **Lenses Packed:** Sigma 20-200mm f/3.5-6.3, SIRUI Aurora 35mm f/1.4, Viltrox AF 85mm f/2.0 Evo
- **Backup Camera:** Sony HX90 (24-720mm equivalent, 1/2.3-inch sensor)
- **Photography Highlights:** Ålesund city centre, Aksla hilltop panorama, Alnes Fyr lighthouse
- **Total Photos Analyzed:** 1,210 (A7C II only, across 5 shooting days)

## **1. Trip Overview and Photography Highlights**

The route from Oslo to Ålesund covers roughly 480 km via the E6 and E136,
passing through the Gudbrandsdal valley — Lillehammer, Vinstra, Dombås — before
descending through the dramatic Romsdal valley toward the coast.
Early April in Norway means spring is barely hinted at: snow still clings
to the mountain passes, bare birch trees line the valleys,
and the light is Nordic-grey for hours on end, punctuated by
frustratingly brief appearances of the sun.

(highlight-alesund-city)=
### 1.1 Ålesund City Centre

Ålesund was rebuilt in Art Nouveau (Jugendstil) style after a devastating fire in 1904.
The town centre is a compact cluster of ornamental stone facades, turrets, and decorative
details that reward both wide-angle storytelling and telephoto detail-hunting.

- **Gear used:** Sigma 20-200mm exclusively
- **Focal lengths:** Mostly 20-35mm for street-level architecture context,
  100-200mm for decorative facade details (gargoyles, ornamental towers, window carvings)
- **Conditions:** Overcast with occasional drizzle; brief 10-minute sun breaks
- **Observation:** The muted, grey-blue light actually complemented the stone facades —
  harsh sunlight would have created distracting shadow patterns on the ornate surfaces.
  The challenge was getting enough colour contrast when everything leaned blue-grey.

(highlight-aksla)=
### 1.2 Aksla Hilltop Panorama

The viewpoint at the top of Aksla (Fjellstua) offers a 360° panorama:
Ålesund town below, the surrounding islands and fjords, and the Sunnmøre Alps
in the distance. 418 steps up from the town centre (or drive).

- **Gear used:** Sigma 20-200mm
- **Focal lengths:** 20mm for the full panorama sweep, 50-100mm for island groupings,
  150-200mm for distant mountain details
- **Conditions:** Heavy overcast with strong wind; ~3 minutes of hazy sun
  that lit the harbour before clouds closed in again
- **Observation:** The brief sunny moment transformed the harbour water
  from flat grey to shimmering blue-green — a completely different mood.
  Having the camera ready at 20mm for the wide context shot,
  then quickly zooming to 100mm for the sun-lit harbour detail,
  was exactly the kind of flexibility the Sigma superzoom excels at.
  No time for lens changes — the light was gone in under three minutes.

(highlight-alnes-fyr)=
### 1.3 Alnes Fyr — Atlantic Coast Lighthouse

Alnes Fyr sits at the western tip of Godøy island, facing the open North Atlantic.
The lighthouse, the rocky shoreline, and the crashing waves make a dramatic
subject — especially in rough weather.

- **Gear used:** Sigma 20-200mm
- **Focal lengths:** 20mm for lighthouse-with-coastline context,
  35-50mm for the lighthouse isolated against sky,
  100-200mm for wave details crashing against rocks
- **Conditions:** Strong wind, intermittent rain, briefly clearing to reveal
  moody grey clouds with bright edges — the most dramatic light of the trip
- **Observation:** This was the one location where 300mm+ would have been useful
  for distant ships on the horizon and seabird details.
  But as noted below, 200mm (300mm equivalent in APS-C crop) felt sufficient
  for telling the story — the compelling frame was always the lighthouse
  against the dramatic sky, not the distant details.

## **2. Gear Usage: What Worked and What Stayed in the Bag**

(sigma-20-200-field-notes)=
### 2.1 Sigma 20-200mm — Daytime Workhorse (Again)

**Verdict: Essential. Never left the camera during daylight hours.**

**The data confirms it:** across 1,210 photos, the Sigma's full 20-200mm range was exercised
continuously. 20mm accounted for 28% of shots (panoramas, street context),
while 200mm was hit 62 times (5.1%) — the Sigma's maximum was a frequently used ceiling,
not a rarely touched extreme. The 100-200mm telephoto range totalled ~14% of all shots.

Consistent with the [Luoyang field report](202603_luoyang_post_purchase_usage.md), the Sigma 20-200mm
once again proved that a single superzoom is the right daytime strategy for solo travel.
In Ålesund's compact town centre, you constantly alternate between wide street scenes (20mm)
and telephoto details on building facades (150-200mm) — the zoom range
eliminates all lens-swap friction.

**Norway-specific observations:**

- **20mm at the Aksla viewpoint** captured the sprawling archipelago panorama
  that made the climb worth it. A 24mm or 25mm start would have missed
  the full impact of the view — the 20mm advantage is real on wide vistas.
- **100-200mm in the town centre** was invaluable for isolating
  individual Art Nouveau decorative elements high on building facades.
  Without telephoto reach, these details would have been lost to the overall scene.
- **At Alnes Fyr**, the full 20-200mm range told a complete story:
  wide establishing shot of lighthouse + coastline, medium shot of the lighthouse alone,
  telephoto close-up of waves crashing on rocks.

> 💡 **Tip — Overcast light is superzoom-friendly.**
> The Sigma's slower aperture (f/5-6.3 at telephoto) is less of a penalty
> under overcast skies than you'd expect: the even, diffused light means
> you rarely need to freeze fast action or fight harsh contrast.
> ISO 800-1600 at f/5.6 and 1/250s handled most daytime telephoto shots cleanly
> on the A7C II. The real aperture limitation only hits after sunset.

(sirui-35-field-notes)=
### 2.2 SIRUI 35mm f/1.4 — After-Sunset Lifesaver

**Verdict: Earned its place. Essential for Ålesund harbour at dusk —
and more useful during the day than expected.**

**The data reveals a surprise:** 35mm was the second most-used focal length on the trip
(240 photos, 19.8%), and 52mm — the SIRUI in APS-C crop mode — added another 74 photos (6.1%).
While some of the 35mm shots were Sigma-at-35mm, the strong 52mm peak confirms
the SIRUI saw significant daytime use in crop mode as a "standard" perspective lens.
April 3 was particularly SIRUI-heavy: 41% at 35mm + 15% at 52mm.

Once the daylight faded (which happens early on overcast April days in Norway —
useful light was gone by 19:30-20:00), the Sigma became unusable for handheld work.
The SIRUI at f/1.4 picked up where the zoom left off.

**Key moments:**

- **Ålesund harbour at dusk:** The coloured building facades along Brosundet canal
  are Ålesund's signature image. At dusk, warm artificial light from restaurants
  and street lamps reflects off the water. SIRUI at f/1.4, ISO 1600, 1/30s with IBIS
  produced sharp, atmospheric shots that the Sigma couldn't have captured
  without a tripod.
- **Post-sunset sky:** The 20 minutes after sunset, when the sky retains
  a deep blue-purple hue behind the Art Nouveau silhouettes,
  is prime "blue hour" territory. f/1.4 handled this comfortably at ISO 2000-3200.

> 💡 **Tip — Pack the SIRUI even for "daytime-only" trips in Nordic spring.**
> Norwegian spring days are deceptively short in terms of *useful photographic light*.
> Heavy overcast can make 17:00 feel like dusk. The SIRUI's f/1.4 extends your
> shooting window by 1-2 hours beyond what the Sigma can handle —
> and those extra hours often produce the moodiest, most atmospheric images of the trip.

> 💡 **New workflow discovery — SIRUI 35mm in APS-C crop mode as a daytime 52mm lens.**
> The 52mm peak in the focal length data (74 photos, 6.1%) was unexpected.
> It indicates you used the SIRUI's APS-C crop mode during the day
> as a quick "standard" perspective — 52mm gives a natural, undistorted field of view
> that's closer to human vision than 35mm.
> This avoids swapping back to the Sigma for moderate-telephoto shots,
> and the SIRUI at f/1.4 in crop mode (14 MP) still delivers excellent quality.
> **This is a valuable addition to the [two-prime workflow](202603_luoyang_post_purchase_usage.md):**
> the SIRUI effectively doubles as a 35mm f/1.4 AND a 52mm f/1.4,
> reducing the gap between 35mm and 85mm in your prime kit.

(viltrox-85-field-notes)=
### 2.3 Viltrox 85mm f/2.0 Evo — Never Used

**Verdict: Dead weight on this trip. Leave it at home for solo trips
in low-season Nordic destinations.**

The Viltrox 85mm is a portrait lens. Portraits require subjects.
On a solo Easter road trip through western Norway in 5°C drizzle,
there were almost no people on the streets — and the few who were
weren't the kind of visually striking subjects (cosplayers, street performers)
that justify pulling out an 85mm prime.

**Lesson learned:**

- The 85mm excels when there are **interesting people to photograph**:
  cosplayer events, street performers, bustling markets, travel companions.
- Solo travel in off-season Nordic conditions offers **none of these**.
- Carrying the Viltrox added 298g of unused weight to every outing.
- **Decision rule:** Only pack the 85mm when you expect human subjects
  (group travel, events, high-season destinations with street life).

(3-hx90-retirement-test)=
## **3. HX90 Retirement Test: A7C II + Sigma vs HX90 at Extreme Telephoto**

This test was designed to answer a specific question from the
[lens recommendation analysis](../gears/lens_recommendation.md):
**Can the A7C II + Sigma 20-200mm fully replace the HX90 at telephoto?**

(test-methodology)=
### 3.1 Test Methodology

Two comparison points were tested, each under three lighting scenarios:

| Test       | A7C II + Sigma Setup                                                                                    | HX90 Setup        |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| **Test 1** | 200mm, APS-C crop mode → **300mm equivalent** (14 MP)                                                   | Zoom to **300mm** |
| **Test 2** | 200mm, APS-C crop mode → 300mm equivalent, then **~2.4× digital enlargement on PC** → simulating ~720mm | Zoom to **720mm** |

Three lighting scenarios for each test:

| Scenario              | Description                                             |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bright outdoor**    | Overcast daylight (~5°C, no direct sun but ample light) |
| **Well-lit indoor**   | Interior room with electric lighting, no window light   |
| **Poorly-lit indoor** | Dim interior, minimal artificial light                  |

(test-results)=
### 3.2 Results

**Test 1 — At 300mm equivalent: A7C II + Sigma wins decisively.**

| Scenario              | Winner             | Margin                                                                                |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bright outdoor**    | **A7C II + Sigma** | Not even comparable — full-frame sensor + quality optics vs 1/2.3-inch compact sensor |
| **Well-lit indoor**   | **A7C II + Sigma** | Same — dramatically sharper, less noise, better colour                                |
| **Poorly-lit indoor** | **A7C II + Sigma** | Even more pronounced — HX90 falls apart at high ISO                                   |

**This was expected.** At the same focal length, a full-frame sensor with a quality lens
should always outperform a tiny 1/2.3-inch compact sensor. The A7C II + Sigma
produces a 14 MP crop that is dramatically sharper, with less noise, better dynamic range,
and more accurate colour than the HX90 at 300mm.

> **Conclusion for Test 1:** If 300mm is enough reach, the HX90 is completely redundant.
> The Sigma in APS-C crop mode delivers a 300mm-equivalent image that is in a different
> quality league from the HX90 at the same focal length.

**Test 2 — At 720mm equivalent: HX90 fights back.**

| Scenario              | Winner             | Margin                                                                                        |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bright outdoor**    | **HX90**           | ~2× sharper / more resolved than Sigma 300mm crop enlarged 2.4× — optical zoom beats digital  |
| **Well-lit indoor**   | **HX90**           | ~2× sharper — same principle: real optical resolution vs interpolated enlargement             |
| **Poorly-lit indoor** | **A7C II + Sigma** | HX90's tiny sensor drowns in noise at high ISO; the Sigma's larger sensor retains more detail |

**This is the critical finding.** At 720mm equivalent, the HX90's real optical zoom
produces genuinely better resolution than digitally enlarging the Sigma's 300mm crop.
The HX90 is optically resolving detail that the Sigma+crop+enlarge pipeline
simply cannot reconstruct — you're comparing real sensor data against interpolated pixels.

The exception is low light: when ISO climbs above ~1600 on the HX90,
its tiny sensor produces so much noise that the noise itself destroys the resolution advantage.
In that scenario, the A7C II's clean high-ISO performance wins even with the enlargement penalty.

(test-analysis)=
### 3.3 Analysis: When to Bring Both Cameras

The test results, combined with the real-world experience on this trip,
lead to a nuanced answer:

**The "300mm is enough" realisation:**

During the entire Ålesund trip, there was not a single moment where
the author genuinely regretted not having 720mm. The compelling photographs
were all captured within the Sigma's native range (20-200mm)
or its APS-C crop range (up to 300mm equivalent).
Even at Alnes Fyr, where distant ships and seabirds could have been subjects,
the story was told effectively at 200mm.

This suggests that **for city and coastal landscape photography,
300mm is a practical ceiling** — beyond which you're chasing details
that don't materially improve the photograph.

**When 720mm is still worth carrying the HX90:**

| Scenario                                       | Bring HX90? | Rationale                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **City / architecture travel**                 | ❌ No        | 300mm APS-C crop from Sigma is more than enough; HX90 adds weight without real benefit                |
| **Coastal landscapes**                         | ❌ No        | The story is in the wide/medium view, not pixel-level distant details                                 |
| **Nature / wildlife safari (e.g. Svalbard)**   | ✅ Yes       | Wildlife at 50-100m+ needs 500-720mm; HX90's optical zoom is irreplaceable here in good daylight      |
| **Bird photography at lakes/wetlands**         | ✅ Yes       | Birds are small and distant; 300mm is rarely enough for frame-filling shots                           |
| **Sports from spectator stands (e.g. diving)** | ✅ Yes       | Confirmed by [diving school analysis](../gears/lens_recommendation.md) — 15-30m distance needs 400mm+ |
| **Solo trip, weight-sensitive, good weather**  | ⚠️ Maybe     | If you know specific 500mm+ subjects exist (whales, distant wildlife), bring it; otherwise skip       |
| **Rainy / overcast / low-light trip**          | ❌ No        | HX90's advantage disappears in poor light; stick with A7C II + Sigma for better high-ISO performance  |

**The bottom-line decision framework:**

```
Do you expect subjects beyond 300mm that you MUST capture in detail?
├── No → Leave HX90 at home. Sigma APS-C crop handles 300mm.
└── Yes → Will the light be good (daylight, well-lit)?
    ├── Yes → Bring HX90. Its optical 720mm genuinely outresolves digital crop.
    └── No → Leave HX90. Its tiny sensor fails in poor light anyway.
```

> 💡 **The psychological takeaway from this trip:**
> "When I can record distant things at 300mm, I actually don't really regret
> that I couldn't take more detailed pictures at 720mm."
> This was a genuine, field-validated observation — not a theoretical compromise.
> For most travel photography, the *story* lives within 300mm.
> Beyond that, you're chasing technical capability, not better photographs.

> 💡 **When HX90 still justifies its existence:**
> Despite the "300mm is enough" finding for *this type of trip*,
> the HX90 remains valuable as a **purpose-specific tool** for nature/wildlife outings
> where extreme telephoto is genuinely needed AND daylight is available.
> It also outperforms any smartphone at telephoto — so when you don't bring
> the A7C II at all, the HX90 in a jacket pocket still delivers better
> telephoto images than the Samsung S25's 69mm 3× zoom.

## **4. Colour Grading for Norwegian Spring: Why the Existing Presets Didn't Work**

The three-preset scheme from the [colour grading article](../tips/color_grading.md) was designed
around **subject-based** classification:

| Preset            | Base | Designed For       |
| ----------------- | ---- | ------------------ |
| 1: Portrait Warm  | FL   | Portraits, people  |
| 2: City Vivid     | IN   | Daytime cityscapes |
| 3: Landscape Bold | IN   | Nature landscapes  |

In practice, these presets struggled on the Ålesund trip for four reasons:

(why-presets-struggled)=
### 4.1 Why the Existing Presets Struggled

**Problem 1: City and nature are inter-weaved in Norway.**

Ålesund is not Oslo or Tokyo. Norwegian towns are small, surrounded by mountains,
fjords, and ocean. A single composition from Aksla includes the town centre,
harbour, surrounding islands, distant snow-capped peaks, and sky — it's simultaneously
"city" and "landscape." Switching between Preset 2 ("City Vivid") and Preset 3
("Landscape Bold") for the same viewpoint makes no sense.

**Problem 2: Volatile weather defeats subject-based classification.**

Within a 30-minute walk through Ålesund, the light changed from flat overcast
to drizzle to a brief burst of sun and back. The *subject* didn't change
(same street, same buildings), but the *light* changed dramatically.
A subject-based preset stays the same; the light-based need changes constantly.

**Problem 3: Low contrast and desaturated scenes.**

Norwegian spring under heavy overcast produces images with remarkably little
tonal separation and muted colours. Preset 2 ("City Vivid" — IN-based, Contrast +1,
Saturation +1) wasn't punchy enough to compensate. Preset 3 ("Landscape Bold" —
Contrast +2, Saturation +2) was closer, but its aggressive Clarity +3
made overcast skies look unnaturally gritty without actually improving the scene.

**Problem 4: Cold colour cast even in sunlight.**

Even during the brief sunny moments, the light had a cool blue quality
quite different from Mediterranean or Asian sunlight. The existing presets
assume "sunny = warm" — but Nordic sun in April, low on the horizon
and filtered through marine atmosphere, carries a distinctly cool bias.
Neither Preset 2 nor 3 accounted for this.

(new-preset-scheme)=
### 4.2 A New Five-Preset Scheme: Lighting-Condition Based

Instead of classifying by *what you're photographing* (city vs landscape vs portrait),
classify by *how the light behaves*. This approach works better in environments
where subjects are mixed but lighting conditions are the primary variable.

**Proposed scheme:**

| Slot | Preset Name                    | Base | Primary Use                                      |
| ---- | ------------------------------ | ---- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| 1    | **Portrait**                   | FL   | People, companions, strangers, self-portraits    |
| 2    | **Outdoor Sunny**              | IN   | Bright daylight — clear or broken cloud          |
| 3    | **Outdoor Cloudy / Overcast**  | FL   | Flat, diffused light — the Nordic default        |
| M1   | **City Nightscape**            | FL   | After-dark city scenes, artificial lighting      |
| M2   | **Indoor Museum / Still Life** | FL   | Museums, galleries, indoor exhibits, restaurants |

> 💡 **Key change: the outdoor presets split on *weather*, not *subject*.**
> In Norway (and Northern Europe generally), whether it's sunny or overcast
> changes *everything* about the image — contrast, saturation, colour temperature,
> dynamic range. The subject (city, landscape, coast) matters far less
> to the colour rendering than the quality of the light falling on it.

(feasibility-evaluation)=
### 4.3 Feasibility Evaluation

**Is a five-preset scheme practical on the A7C II?**

The A7C II supports three Custom Shooting Sets (M1, M2, M3) on the mode dial,
plus additional memory recall (MR) slots via the menu.
A five-preset scheme requires registering five different Creative Look configurations.

**Practical implementation:**

| Method                            | Slots Available | Suitability                                                |
| --------------------------------- | --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mode dial M1 / M2 / M3**        | 3               | Assign the three most-used presets for instant dial access |
| **MR Memory Recall (menu-based)** | 4+ additional   | Slower to access, suitable for less-used presets           |

**Recommended assignment for Nordic travel:**

| Dial / Slot | Preset                     | Rationale                                                     |
| ----------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **M1**      | Portrait                   | Ready for people opportunities when they arise                |
| **M2**      | Outdoor Sunny              | Quick switch for sun breaks — second most used                |
| **M3**      | Outdoor Cloudy / Overcast  | Most frequently needed in Nordic spring — instant dial access |
| **MR 1**    | City Nightscape            | Used only after dark — menu access is acceptable              |
| **MR 2**    | Indoor Museum / Still Life | Used only indoors — menu access is acceptable                 |

**Verdict: Fully feasible.** The three most time-sensitive presets live on the mode dial,
and the two situation-specific ones are accessible through the menu
when the shooting pace allows a few extra seconds.

(preset-parameters)=
### 4.4 Detailed Preset Parameters

All presets below are designed with the Hasselblad-adjacent philosophy noted in the project TODO:
**accuracy and natural rendering as the foundation**, with targeted adjustments
for each lighting condition rather than heavy-handed creative looks.
The base creative looks (FL and IN) are chosen per preset
to complement each lighting scenario.

(new-preset-1-portrait)=
#### Preset 1: Portrait

**Base Look: FL** | **When:** Anytime you're photographing people.

This preset is essentially unchanged from the existing
{ref}`Portrait Warm <preset-1-portrait-warm>` preset —
portrait rendering doesn't change with geography.

| Parameter           | Value  | Rationale                                           |
| ------------------- | ------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Contrast**        | **−2** | Soft skin rendering, reduced harsh shadows on faces |
| **Highlights**      | **−1** | Protects skin highlights from blowing out           |
| **Shadows**         | **+1** | Opens up under-chin and eye-socket shadows          |
| **Fade**            | **1**  | Lifts deepest blacks for an approachable feel       |
| **Saturation**      | **−1** | Prevents skin from going orange under FL's warmth   |
| **Sharpness**       | **0**  | Minimum — smooth skin texture                       |
| **Sharpness Range** | **1**  | Minimum — default                                   |
| **Clarity**         | **0**  | Minimum — smoothest skin rendering                  |

**White Balance:**

| Condition        | WB Mode  | Kelvin     | WB Shift   |
| ---------------- | -------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| Outdoor daylight | Daylight | 5600K      | A+1        |
| Indoor           | Custom K | 4000-4500K | A+1 to A+2 |
| Nordic overcast  | Cloudy   | 6000K      | A+1        |

(new-preset-2-outdoor-sunny)=
#### Preset 2: Outdoor Sunny

**Base Look: IN** | **When:** Clear sky, broken cloud with sun, or any moment of direct sunlight.

IN's natural vibrancy complements the brighter, higher-contrast conditions
of sunny weather. This preset is more moderate than the old "Landscape Bold" —
it works for both city architecture and natural scenery in good light.

| Parameter           | Value  | Rationale                                                               |
| ------------------- | ------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Contrast**        | **+1** | Moderate punch — sunny light already has good contrast; don't overdo it |
| **Highlights**      | **−2** | Protects bright sky, white building facades, and snow on distant peaks  |
| **Shadows**         | **+1** | Opens shadow detail in narrow streets and under tree canopy             |
| **Fade**            | **0**  | Solid blacks — keep the structure in sunny conditions                   |
| **Saturation**      | **+1** | Gentle boost — IN is already vibrant; +1 is enough in good Nordic light |
| **Sharpness**       | **1**  | Crisps up architectural edges and landscape textures                    |
| **Sharpness Range** | **1**  | Fine detail emphasis                                                    |
| **Clarity**         | **2**  | Emphasises building surfaces and landscape depth                        |

**White Balance:**

| Condition                          | WB Mode  | Kelvin | WB Shift | Notes                                                     |
| ---------------------------------- | -------- | ------ | -------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strong sun (rare in Nordic spring) | Daylight | 5600K  | None     | Standard — let IN handle the mood                         |
| Nordic sun (low, cool-tinted)      | Daylight | 5600K  | A+1      | Counters the cool blue bias of Nordic sunlight            |
| Brief sun break through clouds     | Cloudy   | 6000K  | None     | Cloudy WB adds slight warmth to compensate for cool light |

> 💡 **Nordic sun tip:** Even when the sun appears in Norway in April,
> it's low on the horizon (~25-35° altitude at midday) and the atmosphere
> scatters more blue light than at lower latitudes. A subtle A+1 amber shift
> brings Nordic sunlit images closer to the "warm sunshine" feeling
> that the same preset would naturally produce in southern Europe.

(new-preset-3-outdoor-cloudy)=
#### Preset 3: Outdoor Cloudy / Overcast

**Base Look: FL** | **When:** Overcast, flat light, drizzle, fog — the Nordic default.

This is the **most important preset for Nordic travel** and the most different
from the existing scheme. FL is chosen over IN because overcast Nordic light
is inherently cool and flat — FL's warm character counteracts the blue-grey cast,
while IN's vivid punch can look garish when applied to genuinely desaturated scenes.

| Parameter           | Value  | Rationale                                                                          |
| ------------------- | ------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Contrast**        | **+1** | Counteracts the low contrast of flat overcast light — adds needed tonal separation |
| **Highlights**      | **−1** | Moderate — overcast skies rarely blow out, but white clouds need some protection   |
| **Shadows**         | **+1** | Opens up the shadows that tend to block up under flat light                        |
| **Fade**            | **0**  | Keep blacks solid — overcast images already lack punch; don't soften them further  |
| **Saturation**      | **+1** | Compensates for the naturally desaturated overcast palette                         |
| **Sharpness**       | **1**  | Adds definition that flat light removes from edges                                 |
| **Sharpness Range** | **1**  | Fine detail emphasis                                                               |
| **Clarity**         | **2**  | Adds midtone texture and depth — critical for overcast scenes that look "flat"     |

**White Balance — the critical setting for this preset:**

| Condition                        | WB Mode | Kelvin     | WB Shift | Notes                                                             |
| -------------------------------- | ------- | ---------- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Heavy overcast / grey sky**    | Cloudy  | 6000K      | A+1      | Cloudy WB + amber shift counteracts the cold blue-grey cast       |
| **Light overcast / bright grey** | Cloudy  | 6000K      | None     | Less blue cast — Cloudy WB alone is sufficient                    |
| **Drizzle / rain**               | Shade   | 6500-7000K | A+1      | Rain amplifies blue cast; Shade WB + amber provides needed warmth |
| **Fog / mist**                   | Cloudy  | 6000K      | A+1      | Fog is naturally cool; same strategy as heavy overcast            |

> 💡 **Why FL and not IN for overcast?**
> IN amplifies *what's already there*. Under overcast Nordic light,
> "what's there" is desaturated blue-grey — IN makes it *vibrantly* desaturated blue-grey,
> which looks unnatural. FL *introduces* warmth that counters the cool cast,
> producing a palette that feels more like how you *experience* the scene
> (your eyes adapt to the cold light; the camera doesn't).
>
> The Contrast +1 and Clarity +2 provide the structural punch that IN would have given,
> but on FL's warmer tonal foundation.

> ⚠️ **Handling the sun-break transition:**
> Nordic weather can switch from overcast to sunny in minutes.
> When the sun breaks through, the FL-based overcast preset will look noticeably warm —
> fine for a quick shot, but if the sun persists for more than a few minutes,
> switch to Preset 2 (Outdoor Sunny / IN-based) for cleaner, punchier results.
> On the A7C II mode dial (M2 = Sunny, M3 = Overcast), this is a one-click switch.

### 4.6 When to Use Which Scheme

The new five-preset scheme doesn't *replace* the original three — it *complements* it
for different geographies and travel conditions:

| Condition                                                      | Recommended Scheme                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **Southern Europe / Asia (warm, sunny, distinct city/nature)** | Original 3-preset (subject-based)          |
| **Northern Europe / Nordic (cool, volatile, mixed scenes)**    | New 5-preset (lighting-based)              |
| **Mixed trip (e.g. Oslo city + Lofoten nature)**               | New 5-preset                               |
| **Dedicated nature/landscape trip (stable weather)**           | Original Preset 3 (Landscape Bold) is fine |
| **Dedicated city trip (warm climate, stable light)**           | Original Preset 2 (City Vivid) is fine     |

> 💡 **The underlying principle:**
> When the lighting conditions vary more than the subjects, organise presets by light.
> When the subjects vary more than the lighting, organise presets by subject.
> In Norway in spring, the light varies far more than the subject matter —
> hence the lighting-based scheme.

## **5. Practical Tips for Nordic Road Trip Photography**

A few field-learned observations specific to photographing in Norway during spring:

(nordic-weather-strategy)=
### 5.1 Weather-Adaptive Shooting Strategy

- **Check yr.no obsessively.** Norway's weather service (yr.no) provides hyper-local,
  hour-by-hour forecasts with remarkable accuracy. On this trip, it correctly predicted
  a 15-minute sun window at Aksla — time enough to be in position and ready.
- **Shoot through the grey.** Don't wait for sun. Most of the trip's best images
  were under overcast skies: the even light flatters architecture, eliminates harsh shadows,
  and produces moody, atmospheric landscapes. The grey is the light — use it.
- **Be ready for sun breaks.** When the sun appears, you have minutes, not hours.
  Keep the camera accessible (Peak Design Capture Clip), not buried in a backpack.
  The Sigma 20-200mm's single-lens convenience is invaluable here —
  no time for lens swaps when the light is fleeting.
- **Rain is not a stop signal.** Light drizzle produces beautiful saturated colours
  on wet stone, roads, and foliage. The A7C II is weather-resistant.
  Wipe the front element periodically and keep shooting.
