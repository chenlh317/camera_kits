(ningxia-gansu-executive-summary)=
## 📋 Executive Summary

**A 5-day summer trip across two provinces with my cousin and her daughter — and the first real
field test of the second-hand Sony 55mm f/1.8 ZA as a "trip portrait" lens.** This note is the
field diary behind the strategy article {doc}`../tips/standard_lens_and_kit_strategy`: that piece
argues *why* the 55mm belongs in the kit; this one records *what actually happened* when I carried it.

Key takeaways:

1. **660 A7C II photos in 5 days**, and the focal-length data tells a clear two-personality story:
   wide-angle scenery (**~25% at 20mm**) and a deliberate **55mm portrait push on the desert day**
   (17.5% of that day's shots at 55mm — see {ref}`the desert-day test <ningxia-55-test>`).
2. **The 55mm f/1.8 delivered on its promise** exactly where {doc}`../tips/standard_lens_and_kit_strategy`
   predicted: golden-hour, two-people frames in the Tengri desert where f/1.8 keeps one face sharp
   while the second stays recognizable (rather than dissolving, as it would at f/1.4).
   Day 2 split cleanly by tool — the **Sigma zoom for 西夏陵 by day** (its 20mm and 200mm peaks), the
   **55mm for the desert** in the evening and at dawn.
3. **But the "dead zone" is real.** Outside the one day I *consciously* tested it, the 55mm barely
   left the bag — on the low-key drive day I defaulted straight back to the 35mm (92% of that day's
   frames). The lens rewards intention, not habit.
4. **The SIRUI 35mm f/1.4 was the indoor workhorse** at the 甘肃省博物馆 (Gansu Provincial Museum),
   and its APS-C crop mode (52mm-equiv) again earned its keep — 13.8% of the museum day.
5. **The Viltrox 85mm stayed home**, and I didn't miss it: the 55mm stood in for portraits exactly
   as the {ref}`2-lens people-trip kit <standard-lens-example-kits>` predicts.
6. **A 3-lens kit (one zoom + two primes) was the right ceiling** for a multi-terrain trip spanning
   city, museum, desert, and red-rock landscape.

## Trip Context

Five days, two provincial capitals, and about as much terrain variety as a Chinese summer trip can
pack in: the arid plains and imperial tombs outside 银川, a night in a cabin in the 腾格里沙漠, the
Yellow River cutting through 兰州, and a guided morning in a Danxia (丹霞) landform park before the
journey home. This was not a photography expedition — it was a **holiday with family**, and the
camera had to fit around that. That framing matters, because it is exactly the kind of relaxed,
people-first trip the {doc}`../tips/standard_lens_and_kit_strategy` article was written around.

One detail mattered more than I expected: **there were no small children on this trip** — just my
cousin and her 17-year-old daughter. That made *swapping lenses genuinely practical*. With no toddler
to chase and no fear of a decisive moment vanishing while I fumbled a lens change, I could stop, swap
from the Sigma to the 55mm, and take my time composing — the exact freedom a trip with young kids
would have denied me. Much of the deliberate 55mm work below only happened *because* the pace allowed
it.

**Key findings:**

1. **The 55mm push was surgical, not habitual.** Of 34 native-55mm frames across the whole trip,
   virtually all landed in the **腾格里沙漠 on Day 2** (34 at 55mm + 10 at 82mm crop). Every other
   day, the 55mm sat idle. This is the "dead zone" from {doc}`../tips/photographing_tips` behaving
   exactly as documented — the middle focal length only appears when I *decide* to reach for it.
2. **Day 2 was two shoots in one day, split by tool.** The 36%-at-20mm and 9%-at-200mm peaks are the
   **Sigma zoom at 西夏陵** (wide tombs + distant compression); the 55mm cluster is the **desert**
   session. The wide/tele frames are *not* desert shots — they're the daytime mausoleum, a different
   subject and a different lens. That two-phase split is why the day's median lands at 51mm rather
   than at either extreme.
3. **Day 3's 92%-at-35mm** is the flip side: on a low-stakes transit day I reverted to the most
   comfortable prime and never swapped. Honest confirmation that habit beats intention when I'm
   tired.
4. **20mm remains the anchor** (24.9% overall), carried by the wide-scenery segments — 西夏陵 on
   Day 2 and the Danxia park on Day 5.
5. **The S25's 23mm did the "always-with-me" work** (14% of all frames) — the ultra-wide and casual
   documentation the {ref}`gear-complete argument <standard-lens-gear-complete>` says the phone
   should own.

(ningxia-55-test)=
## In-Field Test · The 55mm as a "Trip Portrait" Lens

**The question ({ref}`posed in the strategy article <standard-lens-composing>`):** can a normal
lens in my documented 40-80mm "dead zone" actually become a go-to travel-portrait tool for shooting
my cousin and her daughter — or does it just ride in the bag?

**Methodology.** I reserved the 腾格里沙漠 session (Day 2, from the after-sunset arrival through the
desert overnight) as a dedicated 55mm shoot: two subjects, mostly at f/1.8–f/2.5, on the dunes with
clean, distant backgrounds. The *same day's* daytime frames at 西夏陵 were shot on the Sigma — so
Day 2 is really two shoots, one lens each. Everywhere else on the trip I let the 55mm compete on its
own merits with the zoom and the 35mm.

**Results.**

- **Concentration:** all 34 native-55mm frames + 10 at 82mm (55mm in APS-C crop) sit in Day 2's
  data, and essentially all of them are the **desert session** — *not* the day's 20mm/200mm frames,
  which were the Sigma at 西夏陵. When I committed to the 55mm it delivered volume; on every other
  day it produced almost nothing.
- **f/1.8 keeps the second face recognizable.** The strategy article's core argument
  ({ref}`"the sensible fast" <standard-lens-what-i-love>`) held up in the field — with one caveat on
  the wording. At portrait distance f/1.8 does *not* keep two faces at different distances both
  critically sharp; what it does is keep the face I focus on sharp while my cousin's or her
  daughter's *other* face — a little nearer or farther — stays clearly **recognizable** instead of
  melting into blur. At f/1.4 or f/1.2 that off-plane face would dissolve. That forgiveness, not
  "both sharp," is the real win, and it's the single most useful thing I learned.
- **Rendering:** the ZEISS warmth and gentle roll-off flattered skin in low, warm desert light, and
  paired naturally with the **FL (Film)** Creative Look (see {doc}`../tips/color_grading`).
- **The family loved the results.** This surprised me most: my cousin and her daughter genuinely
  preferred the 55mm frames over anything from a phone. Two things stood out to them — the lens's
  characteristic, **slightly nostalgic colour rendering**, and the **perspective**, which reads
  differently from the ultra-wide ~23mm they're used to on a mobile phone. A 55mm frame simply
  *looks* like a photograph rather than a snapshot, and non-photographers feel that even if they
  can't name why.
- **What I liked as the shooter:** the **light body** (281g — it never unbalanced the A7C II, the
  opposite of the times the SIRUI f/1.4 front-weighted the body on the
  {ref}`Luoyang trip <field-pain-points>`) and the **fast, decisive autofocus** — Eye-AF that locks
  and stays locked, which is what makes candid people frames actually keepers rather than near-misses.

**Conclusion.** The 55mm is a genuine travel-portrait lens **for me — conditionally.** It works
brilliantly when I treat it as a decision (pick a subject, move my feet, commit), and it disappears
when I don't. The desert session validated {ref}`Part 2's playbook <standard-lens-techniques>`; the
92%-at-35mm transit day is the cautionary counter-example. The honest finding isn't about the glass —
it's that I still have to *choose* 55mm, every time.

> 💡 **Rule of thumb going forward:** the 55mm belongs on days with **people + good light + a bit of
> intention**. On pure-scenery or low-energy transit days, don't pretend — leave it in the bag and
> save the weight.

## Color-Grading Reflections

Two very different lighting worlds this trip, and the existing presets in {doc}`../tips/color_grading`
mapped onto them cleanly:

| Scene                                   | Preset used                                   | How it held up                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Desert golden-hour portraits (55mm)     | {ref}`Portrait Warm (FL) <preset-1-portrait-warm>` | **Excellent.** FL's warmth + fade suited the low sun and the ZEISS rendering    |
| 甘肃省博物馆 dim galleries (SIRUI 35mm)     | {ref}`Portrait Warm (FL) <preset-1-portrait-warm>` / FL indoor | Good — FL's lifted shadows tamed the harsh spot-lit cases                        |
| Danxia (丹霞) red-rock ridges (Sigma 20mm) | {ref}`Landscape Bold (IN) <preset-3-landscape-bold>` + **Cloudy WB** | **Too yellow/amber.** The Cloudy white balance over-warmed an already-red subject — see below |
| 黄河 waterfront + 兰州 city                | {ref}`City Vivid (IN) <preset-2-city-vivid>`   | Solid all-rounder for the mixed river-and-city afternoon                         |

**Why the Danxia frames went too amber.** Day 5 was **cloudy and overcast**, and I set the
**Cloudy** white-balance preset out of habit. That was the mistake. A "Cloudy" WB is designed to
*add warmth* — it assumes the overcast sky is casting a cool, bluish light and pushes the balance
toward amber (roughly 6000–6500K) to neutralise it. That correction is right for a grey street or a
snowy scene, but the Danxia (丹霞) ridges are **already** saturated red, orange, and ochre. Layering a
warming WB on top of an intrinsically warm subject meant the amber had nothing neutral to cancel
against, so it just **stacked**: the reds bloomed into orange, and the pale sand and rock between the
bands lost their neutral grey and drifted yellow. "Landscape Bold" then **amplified** the problem —
its saturation boost pushed the already-warm hues further, tipping the whole frame into a flat,
over-baked amber. The soft, low-contrast overcast light made it worse still, because there were no
bright highlights or deep shadows to break up the monochromatic warmth.

**The fix is counter-intuitive: cool it down.** On an already-warm subject you want a **neutral or
slightly cool** white balance — Daylight (~5200–5600K), AWB, or a custom reading off the grey rock —
so the rock's *own* colour carries the frame instead of a warming filter piling on top of it. Under
overcast light specifically, resist the reflex to "warm up the grey day"; here the grey was doing no
harm, and the warmth was the enemy.

**A candidate refinement worth testing.** Beyond fixing the white balance, the Danxia (丹霞) landforms
sit at a saturation extreme that "Landscape Bold" can push over the edge — the most vivid red ridges
risk clipping into a flat, poster-like red. A gentle **"Arid Earth"** variant on the IN base is worth
trialling next time:

| Parameter    | Suggested start (vs Landscape Bold) | Reasoning                                                          |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Saturation   | **−1 to −2**                        | Keep the reds rich without clipping the brightest stripes          |
| Contrast     | +1                                  | Rebuild ridge separation lost in flat, low-contrast overcast light |
| Highlights   | −1                                  | Protect the pale sand and sky between the red bands                |
| White Balance | **Daylight ~5200–5600K (never Cloudy)** | Stop the ochres stacking into amber on an already-warm subject     |

This is a *candidate*, not a validated preset — I'd want a proper Danxia session to confirm it
before committing an M-slot.

(ningxia-lessons)=
## Lessons Learned

1. **A deliberate portrait session shifts the whole distribution.** Day 2's median leapt to 51mm
   purely because I *committed* to the 55mm — proof that the "dead zone" is a habit, not a
   limitation.
2. **The 55mm replaces the 85mm on a people trip.** Leaving the Viltrox home cost me nothing; the
   {ref}`2-lens people-trip logic <standard-lens-example-kits>` played out exactly as written.
3. **3 lenses was the right ceiling** for a multi-terrain trip — zoom for scenery, 35mm for indoor
   low light, 55mm for portraits. No fourth lens would have earned its weight.
4. **f/1.8 > f/1.4 for two-person frames.** The single clearest field validation of the strategy
   article: keeping the off-plane second face *recognizable* is worth more than one extra stop of
   blur that would dissolve it.
5. **Fatigue beats strategy.** On the transit day I defaulted to the 35mm and never applied the
   playbook. Plan the ambitious lens work for high-energy days.
6. **Don't "warm up" an already-warm subject.** The Cloudy WB baked the overcast Danxia frames into
   amber. On red/ochre landforms, use a neutral-to-cool WB (Daylight/AWB) and let the rock's own
   colour do the work — the reflex to warm a grey day backfires here.

(ningxia-open-questions)=
## Open Questions

- **Can the 55mm break out of the desert?** This trip only proved it in ideal golden-hour, clean-
  background conditions. Does it hold up for *candid* people frames in cluttered city streets, or
  does the middle focal length still feel awkward there?
- **Is an "Arid Earth" preset worth a permanent M-slot,** or is per-shot Lightroom correction enough
  for the handful of Danxia-style trips I'll take?
- **Does the 52mm SIRUI crop overlap the 55mm too much?** *Answered on this trip: no.* I never carry
  both for the same purpose. The **35mm f/1.4 is my environment lens** (and, in 52mm APS-C crop, an
  occasional human-focus option); the **55mm f/1.8 is my human-priority lens** (and, in 82mm crop,
  an occasional distant/detail option). They also differ in every practical dimension —
  weight, maximum aperture, and AF behaviour — so they serve different moods, not the same slot. The
  nominal 52-vs-55mm overlap is a coincidence of numbers, not of use.
- **How often will I actually reserve a "portrait session"?** The 55mm's value hinges on intention;
  future trips will tell whether that discipline sticks or the lens drifts back into the bag.
