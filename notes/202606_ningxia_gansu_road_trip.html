2026-06 Ningxia → Gansu: A People Trip and the 55mm’s First Outing#

  • Date: 21–25 June 2026

  • Route: 银川 Yinchuan (宁夏 Ningxia) → 西夏陵 Western Xia Tombs → 腾格里沙漠 Tengger Desert → 兰州 Lanzhou (甘肃 Gansu) → 黄河 Yellow River waterfront → Danxia (丹霞) landform park near Lanzhou

  • Companions: my cousin and her 17-year-old daughter — a relaxed, people-centric holiday

  • Camera Body: Sony A7C II (Full-Frame, E-Mount)

  • Lenses Packed: Sigma 20-200mm f/3.5-6.3 · SIRUI Aurora 35mm f/1.4 · Sony 55mm f/1.8 ZA (new)

  • Phone: Samsung S25 (ultra-wide + everyday snaps)

  • Total Photos Analyzed: 788 (660 on the A7C II across 5 days, 128 on the S25)

📋 Executive Summary#

A 5-day summer trip across two provinces with my cousin and her daughter — and the first real field test of the second-hand Sony 55mm f/1.8 ZA as a “trip portrait” lens. This note is the field diary behind the strategy article The Sony 55mm f/1.8 ZA & a Travel-Kit Decision Pipeline : that piece argues why the 55mm belongs in the kit; this one records what actually happened when I carried it.

Key takeaways:

  1. 660 A7C II photos in 5 days, and the focal-length data tells a clear two-personality story: wide-angle scenery (~25% at 20mm) and a deliberate 55mm portrait push on the desert day (17.5% of that day’s shots at 55mm — see the desert-day test).

  2. The 55mm f/1.8 delivered on its promise exactly where The Sony 55mm f/1.8 ZA & a Travel-Kit Decision Pipeline predicted: golden-hour, two-people frames in the Tengri desert where f/1.8 keeps one face sharp while the second stays recognizable (rather than dissolving, as it would at f/1.4). Day 2 split cleanly by tool — the Sigma zoom for 西夏陵 by day (its 20mm and 200mm peaks), the 55mm for the desert in the evening and at dawn.

  3. But the “dead zone” is real. Outside the one day I consciously tested it, the 55mm barely left the bag — on the low-key drive day I defaulted straight back to the 35mm (92% of that day’s frames). The lens rewards intention, not habit.

  4. The SIRUI 35mm f/1.4 was the indoor workhorse at the 甘肃省博物馆 (Gansu Provincial Museum), and its APS-C crop mode (52mm-equiv) again earned its keep — 13.8% of the museum day.

  5. The Viltrox 85mm stayed home, and I didn’t miss it: the 55mm stood in for portraits exactly as the 2-lens people-trip kit predicts.

  6. A 3-lens kit (one zoom + two primes) was the right ceiling for a multi-terrain trip spanning city, museum, desert, and red-rock landscape.

Trip Context#

Five days, two provincial capitals, and about as much terrain variety as a Chinese summer trip can pack in: the arid plains and imperial tombs outside 银川, a night in a cabin in the 腾格里沙漠, the Yellow River cutting through 兰州, and a guided morning in a Danxia (丹霞) landform park before the journey home. This was not a photography expedition — it was a holiday with family, and the camera had to fit around that. That framing matters, because it is exactly the kind of relaxed, people-first trip the The Sony 55mm f/1.8 ZA & a Travel-Kit Decision Pipeline article was written around.

One detail mattered more than I expected: there were no small children on this trip — just my cousin and her 17-year-old daughter. That made swapping lenses genuinely practical. With no toddler to chase and no fear of a decisive moment vanishing while I fumbled a lens change, I could stop, swap from the Sigma to the 55mm, and take my time composing — the exact freedom a trip with young kids would have denied me. Much of the deliberate 55mm work below only happened because the pace allowed it.

Key findings:

  1. The 55mm push was surgical, not habitual. Of 34 native-55mm frames across the whole trip, virtually all landed in the 腾格里沙漠 on Day 2 (34 at 55mm + 10 at 82mm crop). Every other day, the 55mm sat idle. This is the “dead zone” from Photography Tips Based on Your Usage Patterns behaving exactly as documented — the middle focal length only appears when I decide to reach for it.

  2. Day 2 was two shoots in one day, split by tool. The 36%-at-20mm and 9%-at-200mm peaks are the Sigma zoom at 西夏陵 (wide tombs + distant compression); the 55mm cluster is the desert session. The wide/tele frames are not desert shots — they’re the daytime mausoleum, a different subject and a different lens. That two-phase split is why the day’s median lands at 51mm rather than at either extreme.

  3. Day 3’s 92%-at-35mm is the flip side: on a low-stakes transit day I reverted to the most comfortable prime and never swapped. Honest confirmation that habit beats intention when I’m tired.

  4. 20mm remains the anchor (24.9% overall), carried by the wide-scenery segments — 西夏陵 on Day 2 and the Danxia park on Day 5.

  5. The S25’s 23mm did the “always-with-me” work (14% of all frames) — the ultra-wide and casual documentation the gear-complete argument says the phone should own.

Day 1 · Arrival in 银川 Yinchuan#

A travel day — flights, transfers, and getting the three of us settled. Only 42 frames, mostly 20mm establishing shots of 银川 and a handful of 35mm street snaps as we found dinner. I mounted the 55mm for a few tentative frames of my cousin and her daughter over the first meal (the 56–57mm entries in the data), mostly to re-learn its rendering before the trip proper began. First impression confirmed: light on the A7C II, quick to lock Eye-AF, and a warmer, gentler roll-off than the SIRUI.

Day 2 · 西夏陵 by Day, 腾格里沙漠 by Night#

The signature day of the trip, and — by design — the day I set aside to test the 55mm as a travel-portrait lens.

西夏陵 Western Xia Tombs#

The 西夏陵 (Western Xia Imperial Tombs) sit on the arid plain west of 银川 under the 贺兰山 Helan Mountains — great earthen tumuli standing alone in a wide, sun-bleached landscape. This is Sigma-at-20mm country: the scale of the tombs against the empty plain only reads at wide angle, and the midday sun was harsh enough that the zoom’s slower aperture cost me nothing. A few 200mm frames isolated a single tomb against the mountains.

腾格里沙漠 — Golden Hour and a Desert Cabin#

We reached the edge of the 腾格里沙漠 (Tengger Desert) after sunset and stayed overnight in a cabin among the dunes. The late light — and the golden hour the next morning — was the ideal stage for the 55mm: warm, directional, low-contrast, with clean dune backgrounds that fall away beautifully at f/1.8. This is where 34 of the trip’s 55mm frames were made. See the in-field test below for the analysis.

Day 3 · Drive to 兰州, Light City Tour#

A long transfer south into 甘肃 and a gentle first look at 兰州 — but not only driving. We stopped at 中卫66号公路 (the Zhongwei “Route 66” desert highway), a photogenic stretch of open road through the sand. I didn’t shoot much there — the light and my energy were both low — so it barely registers in the data, but it was a real stop, not just windscreen time. Overall the day left only 50 frames, and the data is almost comically monotone: 92% at 35mm, 8% at 52mm (the SIRUI in crop). On a tiring travel day I clipped on the 35mm f/1.4 and never took it off — no zoom, no 55mm, no deliberation. It’s a useful reminder that the The Sony 55mm f/1.8 ZA & a Travel-Kit Decision Pipeline playbook only works when I have the energy to apply it; left on autopilot, I default to the one prime I trust most.

Day 4 · 兰州 — 黄河 Waterfront, Museums & 白云观#

The fullest sightseeing day of the trip: a morning outdoors along the Yellow River and around 兰州大学, then an afternoon of three indoor stops. The focal-length split mirrors that structure — Sigma-wide (24% at 20mm) for the morning cityscape, SIRUI 35mm (29% at 35mm + 14% at 52mm crop) for the dim afternoon interiors — with a median of 35mm across the 174-frame day.

Morning · 黄河 Waterfront & 兰州大学#

The morning was a walk along the 黄河 as it runs through the heart of 兰州 — famously the only provincial capital the Yellow River flows right through the centre of. Wide 20mm frames for the river and its bridges, 35mm for the riverside promenade and the three of us strolling. We carried the cityscape mood inland onto the 兰州大学 (Lanzhou University) campus — tree-lined avenues and older teaching buildings that gave the walk a quieter, collegiate register. A relaxed, mixed-focal-length morning (median 35mm) with the Sigma doing the daytime heavy lifting.

Afternoon · Dunhuang Museum, 甘肃省博物馆 & 白云观#

The afternoon was three stops indoors, and the SIRUI 35mm f/1.4’s chance to shine. First the Dunhuang Museum (敦煌-themed artefacts and mural reproductions), then the 甘肃省博物馆 (Gansu Provincial Museum) — home of the famous 铜奔马 (the “Galloping Horse” / Flying Horse of Gansu) — and finally the 白云观 (Baiyun Taoist Temple / Baiyunguan) on the south bank of the Yellow River. Dim gallery lighting, no flash, glass cases, and the shaded temple halls: exactly the low-light indoor scenario the fast prime was bought for. The data backs it up — 29% of the day at 35mm plus 14% at 52mm (the SIRUI’s APS-C crop) — over 40% of the day on that one lens or its crop. The 52mm crop mode again proved its worth for tightening onto a single artefact without swapping to the Sigma, the same workflow the Ålesund trip surfaced.

Day 5 · Danxia (丹霞) Landform Park, Then Home#

The biggest shooting day (200 frames) and the widest: 36% at 20mm, median 31mm. A guided morning through a Danxia (丹霞) landform park near 兰州 — layered red-and-ochre sandstone ridges that are all about scale and colour. Pure Sigma-at-20mm territory, with occasional 200mm reaches to compress distant striped ridgelines. The 55mm never came out: with no intimate people-moments and everything about the landscape, there was no case for it. Then the long journey home.

In-Field Test · The 55mm as a “Trip Portrait” Lens#

The question (posed in the strategy article): can a normal lens in my documented 40-80mm “dead zone” actually become a go-to travel-portrait tool for shooting my cousin and her daughter — or does it just ride in the bag?

Methodology. I reserved the 腾格里沙漠 session (Day 2, from the after-sunset arrival through the desert overnight) as a dedicated 55mm shoot: two subjects, mostly at f/1.8–f/2.5, on the dunes with clean, distant backgrounds. The same day’s daytime frames at 西夏陵 were shot on the Sigma — so Day 2 is really two shoots, one lens each. Everywhere else on the trip I let the 55mm compete on its own merits with the zoom and the 35mm.

Results.

  • Concentration: all 34 native-55mm frames + 10 at 82mm (55mm in APS-C crop) sit in Day 2’s data, and essentially all of them are the desert session — not the day’s 20mm/200mm frames, which were the Sigma at 西夏陵. When I committed to the 55mm it delivered volume; on every other day it produced almost nothing.

  • f/1.8 is the balanced middle. The strategy article’s core argument (“the sensible fast”) held up in the field. f/1.8 sits in a sweet spot: shallow enough to lift the subject’s face off the background and give it that pleasing separation, yet not so shallow that a second face — my cousin’s or her daughter’s, a little nearer or farther — blurs past recognition. At f/1.4 or f/1.2 that off-plane face would dissolve; stopped well down it would flatten into the background. f/1.8 threads the needle.

  • Rendering: the ZEISS warmth and gentle roll-off flattered skin in low, warm desert light, and paired naturally with the FL (Film) Creative Look (see Color Grading: In-Camera & Post-Processing Tips ).

  • The family loved the results. This surprised me most: my cousin and her daughter genuinely preferred the 55mm frames over anything from a phone. Two things stood out to them — the lens’s characteristic, slightly nostalgic colour rendering, and the perspective, which reads differently from the ultra-wide ~23mm they’re used to on a mobile phone. A 55mm frame simply looks like a photograph rather than a snapshot, and non-photographers feel that even if they can’t name why.

  • What I liked as the shooter: the light body (281g — it never unbalanced the A7C II, the opposite of the times the SIRUI f/1.4 front-weighted the body on the Luoyang trip) and the fast, decisive autofocus — Eye-AF that locks and stays locked, which is what makes candid people frames actually keepers rather than near-misses.

Conclusion. The 55mm is a genuine travel-portrait lens for me — conditionally. It works brilliantly when I treat it as a decision (pick a subject, move my feet, commit), and it disappears when I don’t. The desert session validated Part 2’s playbook; the 92%-at-35mm transit day is the cautionary counter-example. The honest finding isn’t about the glass — it’s that I still have to choose 55mm, every time.

💡 Rule of thumb going forward: the 55mm belongs on days with people + good light + a bit of intention. On pure-scenery or low-energy transit days, don’t pretend — leave it in the bag and save the weight.

Why the Danxia frames went too amber. Day 5 was cloudy and overcast, and I set the Cloudy white-balance preset out of habit. That was the mistake. A “Cloudy” WB is designed to add warmth — it assumes the overcast sky is casting a cool, bluish light and pushes the balance toward amber (roughly 6000–6500K) to neutralise it. That correction is right for a grey street or a snowy scene, but the Danxia (丹霞) ridges are already saturated red, orange, and ochre. Layering a warming WB on top of an intrinsically warm subject meant the amber had nothing neutral to cancel against, so it just stacked: the reds bloomed into orange, and the pale sand and rock between the bands lost their neutral grey and drifted yellow. “Landscape Bold” then amplified the problem — its saturation boost pushed the already-warm hues further, tipping the whole frame into a flat, over-baked amber. The soft, low-contrast overcast light made it worse still, because there were no bright highlights or deep shadows to break up the monochromatic warmth.

The fix is counter-intuitive: cool it down. On an already-warm subject you want a neutral or slightly cool white balance — Daylight (~5200–5600K), AWB, or a custom reading off the grey rock — so the rock’s own colour carries the frame instead of a warming filter piling on top of it. Under overcast light specifically, resist the reflex to “warm up the grey day”; here the grey was doing no harm, and the warmth was the enemy.

A candidate refinement worth testing. Beyond fixing the white balance, the Danxia (丹霞) landforms sit at a saturation extreme that “Landscape Bold” can push over the edge — the most vivid red ridges risk clipping into a flat, poster-like red. A gentle “Arid Earth” variant on the IN base is worth trialling next time:

Lessons Learned#

  1. A deliberate portrait session shifts the whole distribution. Day 2’s median leapt to 51mm purely because I committed to the 55mm — proof that the “dead zone” is a habit, not a limitation.

  2. The 55mm replaces the 85mm on a people trip. Leaving the Viltrox home cost me nothing; the 2-lens people-trip logic played out exactly as written.

  3. 3 lenses was the right ceiling for a multi-terrain trip — zoom for scenery, 35mm for indoor low light, 55mm for portraits. No fourth lens would have earned its weight.

  4. f/1.8 > f/1.4 for two-person frames. The single clearest field validation of the strategy article: keeping the off-plane second face recognizable is worth more than one extra stop of blur that would dissolve it.

  5. Fatigue beats strategy. On the transit day I defaulted to the 35mm and never applied the playbook. Plan the ambitious lens work for high-energy days.

  6. Don’t “warm up” an already-warm subject. The Cloudy WB baked the overcast Danxia frames into amber. On red/ochre landforms, use a neutral-to-cool WB (Daylight/AWB) and let the rock’s own colour do the work — the reflex to warm a grey day backfires here.

Open Questions#

  • Can the 55mm break out of the desert? This trip only proved it in ideal golden-hour, clean- background conditions. Does it hold up for candid people frames in cluttered city streets, or does the middle focal length still feel awkward there?

  • Is an “Arid Earth” preset worth a permanent M-slot, or is per-shot Lightroom correction enough for the handful of Danxia-style trips I’ll take?

  • Does the 52mm SIRUI crop overlap the 55mm too much? Answered on this trip: no. I never carry both for the same purpose. The 35mm f/1.4 is my environment lens (and, in 52mm APS-C crop, an occasional human-focus option); the 55mm f/1.8 is my human-priority lens (and, in 82mm crop, an occasional distant/detail option). They also differ in every practical dimension — weight, maximum aperture, and AF behaviour — so they serve different moods, not the same slot. The nominal 52-vs-55mm overlap is a coincidence of numbers, not of use.

  • How often will I actually reserve a “portrait session”? The 55mm’s value hinges on intention; future trips will tell whether that discipline sticks or the lens drifts back into the bag.
