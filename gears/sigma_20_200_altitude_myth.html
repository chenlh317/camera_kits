The Sigma 20-200mm “Altitude Sickness” Claim: An Evidence Check#

A circulating claim in the Chinese-language photography community holds that the Sigma 20-200mm f/3.5-6.3 DG DN Contemporary goes soft — “blurry from near to far” — once you take it to high altitude. This chapter examines that claim against the sample photos that are usually cited, against basic optics and physics, and against how the evidence has actually been presented. It is a reasoning exercise, not an accusation about any specific person.

Summary

The “sample photos” behind this claim do not survive scrutiny. Their softness is fully explained by heavy crop-and-upscale, motion blur at the long end, and atmospheric haze over distance — not by any lens defect. The proposed mechanism (“the barrel is too airtight to cope with air-pressure change”) is physically impossible for a zoom lens. And a genuine optical defect would be reproducible worldwide, yet no authoritative outlet has ever reproduced it. Absent raw files, EXIF, and controlled testing, the claim does not rise to the level of usable negative information.

What the claim says#

The narrative has a few stable, recognisable ingredients:

  • The lens is said to become soft specifically at high altitude (typically the Tibetan Plateau / Qinghai-Gansu high country).

  • The failure is described as uniform — “every shot on the trip was blurry”, often “only noticed after getting home”.

  • A pseudo-scientific mechanism is offered: the lens groups are “sealed too well”, so the barrel “cannot adapt to the pressure change” at altitude, and the image degrades. The catchy shorthand used is the lens catching “altitude sickness” (镜头”高反”).

  • The discussion is confined to one language market; no non-Chinese outlet or reviewer discusses it.

Each ingredient matters, because each is testable.

Reading the sample photos#

The images most often passed around fall into two groups, and the split between them is the single most informative thing about the whole set.

The full-frame originals are actually sharp. A wide salt-lake / Yardang landscape, a snow-mountain-and-tent scene, and a glacier-with-figure shot all resolve fine detail normally — ridge texture, rock strata, water ripples, and distant terrain are all distinguishable, with normal colour and contrast. A lens that had “failed at altitude” could not produce these on the same trip with the same body.

The “problem” images are almost all extreme crops. The pictures labelled as proof — a dancing figure by the water, an arm close-up, a person against a glacier — are small regions cropped out of already-compressed uploads and then enlarged to fill the frame. Their softness is what you get when you magnify a limited number of pixels together with the JPEG compression artefacts. Any lens, including the very best, looks like this when you crop this hard into a social-platform re-compression.

Crucially, none of the real fingerprints of an optical defect appear:

  • Decentering would show one side of the frame soft and the other sharp, consistently across images. It does not appear — the wide originals are even across the frame.

  • Field curvature / corner softness would show a sharp centre with a regular fall-off to the corners. The softness here tracks which region was enlarged, not position in the frame.

  • Uniformly soft across all originals would be the signature of a genuinely poor copy. The opposite is true: multiple full-frame originals are sharp.

  • Runaway lateral CA / purple fringing on high-contrast edges is not systematically present.

So the dominant “blur” was largely manufactured after capture by cropping and enlarging a re-compressed upload, with additional, genuinely-captured contributions from motion (shake / moving subjects) and atmosphere. None of it requires — or points to — a lens defect.

Sample photos

The circulating “sample” images discussed here are available as a download: sample_pics.zip. They carry third-party watermarks / platform IDs and are provided only so the “sharp full-frame original vs. soft extreme-crop” contradiction can be inspected first-hand.

Could it be deliberate defocus?#

A soft “sample” is trivial to manufacture with a perfectly healthy lens: stop down to about f/8, switch to manual focus, and rack the focus ring past the infinity hard-stop without dialling back. Modern focus-by-wire lenses deliberately focus beyond true infinity (to allow for thermal expansion and calibration margin), so the focal plane ends up behind everything in the scene and near and far go soft at once. This matters as an epistemic point: a blurry frame proves almost nothing, because a good copy can be made to look “defective” on demand.

But do the circulating samples actually look like beyond-infinity defocus? No — and the distinction is worth drawing, because defocus has a specific, recognisable signature that these images do not match:

Defocus (past infinity) would show

The samples actually show

Global, uniform softness — every distance out of focus together

Several full-frame originals are properly sharp (salt-lake, snow-mountain tent, turret house, figure on the salt flat)

Isotropic blur — points spread into round discs, edge halos symmetric

Directional streaks — arms with doubled outlines pulled one way, ground smeared along the direction of travel

No moving-vs-static distinction — everything equally soft

Static subjects sharp, movers soft in the same frame (the house’s roof tiles and window frame are crisp while the girl beside it smears)

Two of these are decisive. The directional streaking is the fingerprint of motion, not focus (defocus is non-directional). And the static-sharp / mover-soft frame is self-refuting for any whole-image defocus: if the lens were focused behind the scene, the house would be exactly as blurred as the girl — since it is not, the lens was focused correctly.

So the two arguments are separate and both hold: deliberate defocus shows that “blurry ≠ defective lens,” while the specific circulating images fail to implicate the lens for a different reason — motion blur, extreme crop-and-upscale, and atmospheric haze, exactly as catalogued in the table above.

Why altitude (almost) cannot blur a lens#

The proposed mechanism is the weakest link, and it fails on basic physics.

  • A zoom lens is not sealed — it is effectively an air pump. A 20-200mm superzoom changes internal volume dramatically as the barrel extends and retracts; air is drawn in and pushed out as you zoom (many lenses have dedicated venting / dust structures for exactly this). “Too airtight” is self-contradictory for a lens that breathes air to zoom.

  • Even if it were sealed, the pressure difference cannot blur glass. At ~4000 m the pressure is roughly 60% of sea level. That differential acting on rigid glass elements will not deform their surface figure enough to affect resolution — glass is not a balloon. Any pressure load is on the barrel shell, not on optical focus.

  • What actually changes with altitude is temperature, not image-forming pressure. Cold affects battery life, grease feel, and mechanical tolerances — none of which produce “whole-frame double-imaging and blown highlights”.

  • Thin, dry high-altitude air is usually clearer overall, with less haze and water vapour, so distant subjects tend to be more crisp, not less. (The exception is extreme-telephoto shots across many kilometres — or across ice and water, where local vapour and mist linger — which is exactly the environmental, lens-agnostic softness noted in the table above.)

“Altitude sickness” is a metaphor for people, retrofitted onto a lens. It is technobabble: memorable, easy to spread, and unfalsifiable — but it does not describe a real optical mechanism. A real defect is discussed in concrete, measurable terms (decentering, field curvature, dispersion, an MTF drop at a specific focal length and aperture), not a poetic analogy.

Why “worldwide silence” is the strongest tell#

Optics do not respect borders. If “high altitude → sealed groups → blur” were a real optical phenomenon, it would be reproducible everywhere: the Andes, the Himalaya, the Alps, Colorado, Bolivia. Outlets that run standardized optical tests or large-sample failure data — DPReview, LensTip, teardown channels, and especially rental-house statistics like LensRentals — would have something to say. Instead the phenomenon exists only inside a single language market and vanishes outside it.

A “defect” that appears only in one language community is more naturally a market phenomenon than a physical one. Physics does not pick a language; marketing campaigns do.

Astroturfing vs. genuine feedback: how to tell them apart#

Once a claim looks more like a market phenomenon than a physical one, a fair question follows: how would you distinguish organized smearing (有组织抹黑) from ordinary negative feedback? This section is deliberately general — it is a way of reasoning about any suspiciously one-sided pile-on, not an accusation against any specific person or account. The honest position is that individual posts almost never prove anything; only a pattern of several independent anomalies is informative.

Some signals that, taken together, are more consistent with a coordinated campaign than with spontaneous word-of-mouth:

Signal

Why it is suspicious in aggregate

Sustained, cross-platform complaints for months

Real buyers usually vent in a short burst (get a bad unit → return/exchange → move on). Long, multi-platform campaigning with no personal remedy at stake is an odd use of one’s time.

Complaints that funnel to one competing model

Genuine gripes say “this lens let me down.” They rarely converge, in unison, on the same specific alternative. “Complaint + precise product recommendation” is a marketing move.

No falsifiable evidence, ever

Original RAW/JPEG, EXIF, repair tickets, and support-chat logs are systematically absent — while emotion and confident conclusions are abundant. A wronged buyer usually rushes to show proof.

A catchy, unfalsifiable, pseudo-scientific slogan

A memorable but physically wrong “mechanism” (here, “the lens caught altitude sickness”) spreads for its stickiness, not its correctness.

“Aged but thin” account profiles

Accounts that are years old yet sparse, whose few posts are unrelated, but which carry one or two laser-focused complaint posts. Maturity plus sudden single-topic focus is a known nurtured-account (养号) shape.

Internally contradictory accounts

The story mutates under questioning: “I didn’t realise every shot was blurry until I got home” — yet, when pressed in the comments, the same person says “I even tried switching to manual focus and it was still blurry.” Noticing badly enough to troubleshoot MF on the spot cannot coexist with not noticing until home.

Narrative devices that dodge scrutiny

Framings such as “I only noticed every shot was blurry after I got home” both maximise emotional impact and conveniently explain away the missing on-the-spot parameters — even though real shooters routinely chimp (zoom in to check sharpness) as they shoot.

The decisive variable is still the one from the sections above: is there any reproducible, falsifiable evidence, and does the chorus funnel to a single competitor? A combination of “zero original evidence + tidy funnelling” tilts the scale toward a guided campaign.

Guard against over-reading it, too#

Every signal above also has an innocent explanation, and intellectual honesty requires holding both in view:

  • Memes converge on their own. One early, catchy post can seed a slogan that later posters sincerely believe and repeat — uniform wording does not by itself prove a coordinated script.

  • Language silos are real. Chinese and English communities are naturally separated by platform and language, so a discussion can stay contained within one of them even when the subject matter is not region-specific.

  • Recommendation algorithms and affiliate creators routinely surface a competing product under any popular item’s negative posts — not necessarily the same organized group.

  • Survivorship bias: satisfied owners stay quiet while unhappy ones post repeatedly, which alone concentrates negative voices.

  • Plain internet anger exists: real people can be emotional, vague on parameters, and unwilling to screenshot, without being paid actors.

So “looks like astroturfing” is not “is astroturfing,” and “easy to fabricate” is not “was fabricated.” The useful conclusion does not require identifying anyone: by Occam’s razor, when several orthogonal anomalies stack up — no original evidence, aged-but-thin accounts, a scrutiny-dodging narrative, tidy funnelling to one competitor, a catchy pseudo-scientific slogan, and global non-reproduction — “spontaneous genuine word-of-mouth” becomes the most expensive hypothesis to hold. You never have to prove a campaign; the burden of proof for a defect stays with whoever asserts it.

What would actually count as evidence#

The claim would become worth weighing the moment any of the following appeared — and, notably, they have not:

  1. Unretouched original files (RAW or full-resolution JPEG) with intact EXIF, not screen re-shots or platform re-uploads.

  2. A controlled comparison: tripod, remote/timer release, correct image-stabilisation handling, a static distant target (a ridge, a sign), at both low and high altitude.

  3. EXIF that rules out slow-shutter motion blur — shutter speed at or above the reciprocal of the focal length at the long end.

  4. A consistent, cross-image defect fingerprint (e.g. one-sided softness for decentering) rather than “whatever region was enlarged”.

Note where the burden of proof sits: it is on the party asserting a defect. Until reproducible, falsifiable evidence exists, the softness in the circulating images is better explained by shooting technique and file handling than by the lens.

Why “altitude” reads as motion blur in disguise#

There is a mundane reason high-altitude trips feel like they produce softer long-lens shots — and it has nothing to do with the glass:

  • Slow shutter at the long end. At 200mm the maximum aperture is only f/6.3. In dimmer light the camera drops shutter speed to expose, and hand-holding a 200mm-equivalent frame below its safe shutter (~1/200 s) smears easily.

  • Wind. High, open terrain is windy; both subject and photographer move more.

  • Cold and thin air reduce hand steadiness and attention.

  • Strong backlight / snow glare invite exposure and focus errors.

The real culprit is slow shutter + hand-holding + long focal length, which is easy to misread as “the lens broke at altitude.” This is also why the smeared shots show a moving subject soft while the static background stays sharper — the classic signature of motion blur, not optics. See museum indoor guidance and the long-end habits in Photography Tips Based on Your Usage Patterns for the same slow-shutter trade-off in other settings.

A note from actually owning this lens#

I own this Sigma 20-200mm. It does not deliver superb resolution — it is a very-high-ratio travel zoom, and like all such lenses it is “good enough rather than exceptional”, generally soft wide-open at the extremes. But across my own use it has done its job without optical or mechanical flaws. I have not shot it at high altitude, so my experience cannot disprove a high-altitude-specific claim — and that cuts both ways: my “it’s fine for me” carries no more weight against the claim than an anonymous “it broke for me” carries for it. What settles the question is not either anecdote, but reproducible evidence — which, so far, only one side is missing.

For where this lens fits as a kit-simplifying superzoom (and its documented, real optical trade-offs — 20mm distortion, vignetting, edge softness at 200mm), see Option 1B in Complete Camera Gear Recommendation: Data-Driven Analysis.

Bottom line#

A claimed physical defect that (1) offers no falsifiable original evidence, (2) rests on a mechanism that violates basic optics, and (3) is reproduced by no authoritative outlet and exists in only one language market — does not constitute valid negative information and should not carry weight in a purchase decision. This verdict is about the evidence, not about any individual: even if a genuine bad copy exists somewhere, the burden of proof lies with the party asserting the defect, and the softness in the circulating “samples” is fully accounted for by crop-and-upscale, motion blur, and atmosphere.
