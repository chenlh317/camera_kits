Road Trip Reflections: Oslo to Ålesund — Easter 2026 #

  • Date: April 2026

  • Context: Solo road trip Oslo → Ålesund via Lillehammer, Gjøvik, Dombås

  • Duration: Early April 2026 (Easter break)

  • Weather: Very volatile — mainly cloudy, sometimes rainy, occasionally very brief sunny moments. ~5°C

  • Camera Body: Sony A7C II (Full-Frame, E-Mount)

  • Lenses Packed: Sigma 20-200mm f/3.5-6.3, SIRUI Aurora 35mm f/1.4, Viltrox AF 85mm f/2.0 Evo

  • Backup Camera: Sony HX90 (24-720mm equivalent, 1/2.3-inch sensor)

  • Photography Highlights: Ålesund city centre, Aksla hilltop panorama, Alnes Fyr lighthouse

  • Total Photos Analyzed: 1,210 (A7C II only, across 5 shooting days)

📋 Executive Summary#

A solo Easter road trip through central Norway to the Atlantic coast — testing gear, testing colour presets, and confronting the reality of Nordic spring weather.

This article captures field reflections and practical tips from a drive through some of Norway’s most photogenic scenery under less-than-ideal conditions. Key takeaways:

  1. 1,210 photos in 5 days — substantial output despite volatile weather. Focal length analysis confirms a familiar wide-angle-dominant pattern: 28% at 20mm, 20% at 35mm — these two focal lengths alone account for nearly half of all shots.

  2. The Sigma 20-200mm once again proved its worth as a daytime one-lens solution — from Ålesund’s Art Nouveau architecture to the wild Atlantic coast at Alnes Fyr.

  3. The SIRUI 35mm f/1.4 earned its keep after sunset, delivering usable handheld shots of Ålesund’s harbour at dusk when the Sigma’s slow aperture would have failed. Its APS-C crop mode (52mm equiv) also saw notable daytime use — 6% of all shots.

  4. The Viltrox 85mm and HX90 stayed in the bag the entire trip — solo travel with no companions and sparse foot traffic (thanks to the weather) meant no portrait opportunities, and 300mm APS-C crop from the Sigma satisfied all telephoto needs.

  5. The existing three-preset colour scheme (Portrait Warm / City Vivid / Landscape Bold) struggled in Norwegian spring — the inter-weaving of city and nature, volatile weather, and cold, desaturated light demanded a different approach.

  6. A new five-preset scheme organised by lighting condition rather than subject is proposed and evaluated below — better suited for Nordic and similar environments.

1. Trip Overview and Photography Highlights#

The route from Oslo to Ålesund covers roughly 480 km via the E6 and E136, passing through the Gudbrandsdal valley — Lillehammer, Vinstra, Dombås — before descending through the dramatic Romsdal valley toward the coast. Early April in Norway means spring is barely hinted at: snow still clings to the mountain passes, bare birch trees line the valleys, and the light is Nordic-grey for hours on end, punctuated by frustratingly brief appearances of the sun.

1.1 Ålesund City Centre#

Ålesund was rebuilt in Art Nouveau (Jugendstil) style after a devastating fire in 1904. The town centre is a compact cluster of ornamental stone facades, turrets, and decorative details that reward both wide-angle storytelling and telephoto detail-hunting.

  • Gear used: Sigma 20-200mm exclusively

  • Focal lengths: Mostly 20-35mm for street-level architecture context, 100-200mm for decorative facade details (gargoyles, ornamental towers, window carvings)

  • Conditions: Overcast with occasional drizzle; brief 10-minute sun breaks

  • Observation: The muted, grey-blue light actually complemented the stone facades — harsh sunlight would have created distracting shadow patterns on the ornate surfaces. The challenge was getting enough colour contrast when everything leaned blue-grey.

1.2 Aksla Hilltop Panorama#

The viewpoint at the top of Aksla (Fjellstua) offers a 360° panorama: Ålesund town below, the surrounding islands and fjords, and the Sunnmøre Alps in the distance. 418 steps up from the town centre (or drive).

  • Gear used: Sigma 20-200mm

  • Focal lengths: 20mm for the full panorama sweep, 50-100mm for island groupings, 150-200mm for distant mountain details

  • Conditions: Heavy overcast with strong wind; ~3 minutes of hazy sun that lit the harbour before clouds closed in again

  • Observation: The brief sunny moment transformed the harbour water from flat grey to shimmering blue-green — a completely different mood. Having the camera ready at 20mm for the wide context shot, then quickly zooming to 100mm for the sun-lit harbour detail, was exactly the kind of flexibility the Sigma superzoom excels at. No time for lens changes — the light was gone in under three minutes.

1.3 Alnes Fyr — Atlantic Coast Lighthouse#

Alnes Fyr sits at the western tip of Godøy island, facing the open North Atlantic. The lighthouse, the rocky shoreline, and the crashing waves make a dramatic subject — especially in rough weather.

  • Gear used: Sigma 20-200mm

  • Focal lengths: 20mm for lighthouse-with-coastline context, 35-50mm for the lighthouse isolated against sky, 100-200mm for wave details crashing against rocks

  • Conditions: Strong wind, intermittent rain, briefly clearing to reveal moody grey clouds with bright edges — the most dramatic light of the trip

  • Observation: This was the one location where 300mm+ would have been useful for distant ships on the horizon and seabird details. But as noted below, 200mm (300mm equivalent in APS-C crop) felt sufficient for telling the story — the compelling frame was always the lighthouse against the dramatic sky, not the distant details.

2. Gear Usage: What Worked and What Stayed in the Bag#

2.1 Sigma 20-200mm — Daytime Workhorse (Again)#

Verdict: Essential. Never left the camera during daylight hours.

The data confirms it: across 1,210 photos, the Sigma’s full 20-200mm range was exercised continuously. 20mm accounted for 28% of shots (panoramas, street context), while 200mm was hit 62 times (5.1%) — the Sigma’s maximum was a frequently used ceiling, not a rarely touched extreme. The 100-200mm telephoto range totalled ~14% of all shots.

Consistent with the Luoyang field report, the Sigma 20-200mm once again proved that a single superzoom is the right daytime strategy for solo travel. In Ålesund’s compact town centre, you constantly alternate between wide street scenes (20mm) and telephoto details on building facades (150-200mm) — the zoom range eliminates all lens-swap friction.

Norway-specific observations:

  • 20mm at the Aksla viewpoint captured the sprawling archipelago panorama that made the climb worth it. A 24mm or 25mm start would have missed the full impact of the view — the 20mm advantage is real on wide vistas.

  • 100-200mm in the town centre was invaluable for isolating individual Art Nouveau decorative elements high on building facades. Without telephoto reach, these details would have been lost to the overall scene.

  • At Alnes Fyr, the full 20-200mm range told a complete story: wide establishing shot of lighthouse + coastline, medium shot of the lighthouse alone, telephoto close-up of waves crashing on rocks.

💡 Tip — Overcast light is superzoom-friendly. The Sigma’s slower aperture (f/5-6.3 at telephoto) is less of a penalty under overcast skies than you’d expect: the even, diffused light means you rarely need to freeze fast action or fight harsh contrast. ISO 800-1600 at f/5.6 and 1/250s handled most daytime telephoto shots cleanly on the A7C II. The real aperture limitation only hits after sunset.

2.2 SIRUI 35mm f/1.4 — After-Sunset Lifesaver#

Verdict: Earned its place. Essential for Ålesund harbour at dusk — and more useful during the day than expected.

The data reveals a surprise: 35mm was the second most-used focal length on the trip (240 photos, 19.8%), and 52mm — the SIRUI in APS-C crop mode — added another 74 photos (6.1%). While some of the 35mm shots were Sigma-at-35mm, the strong 52mm peak confirms the SIRUI saw significant daytime use in crop mode as a “standard” perspective lens. April 3 was particularly SIRUI-heavy: 41% at 35mm + 15% at 52mm.

Once the daylight faded (which happens early on overcast April days in Norway — useful light was gone by 19:30-20:00), the Sigma became unusable for handheld work. The SIRUI at f/1.4 picked up where the zoom left off.

Key moments:

  • Ålesund harbour at dusk: The coloured building facades along Brosundet canal are Ålesund’s signature image. At dusk, warm artificial light from restaurants and street lamps reflects off the water. SIRUI at f/1.4, ISO 1600, 1/30s with IBIS produced sharp, atmospheric shots that the Sigma couldn’t have captured without a tripod.

  • Post-sunset sky: The 20 minutes after sunset, when the sky retains a deep blue-purple hue behind the Art Nouveau silhouettes, is prime “blue hour” territory. f/1.4 handled this comfortably at ISO 2000-3200.

💡 Tip — Pack the SIRUI even for “daytime-only” trips in Nordic spring. Norwegian spring days are deceptively short in terms of useful photographic light. Heavy overcast can make 17:00 feel like dusk. The SIRUI’s f/1.4 extends your shooting window by 1-2 hours beyond what the Sigma can handle — and those extra hours often produce the moodiest, most atmospheric images of the trip.

💡 New workflow discovery — SIRUI 35mm in APS-C crop mode as a daytime 52mm lens. The 52mm peak in the focal length data (74 photos, 6.1%) was unexpected. It indicates you used the SIRUI’s APS-C crop mode during the day as a quick “standard” perspective — 52mm gives a natural, undistorted field of view that’s closer to human vision than 35mm. This avoids swapping back to the Sigma for moderate-telephoto shots, and the SIRUI at f/1.4 in crop mode (14 MP) still delivers excellent quality. This is a valuable addition to the two-prime workflow: the SIRUI effectively doubles as a 35mm f/1.4 AND a 52mm f/1.4, reducing the gap between 35mm and 85mm in your prime kit.

2.3 Viltrox 85mm f/2.0 Evo — Never Used#

Verdict: Dead weight on this trip. Leave it at home for solo trips in low-season Nordic destinations.

The Viltrox 85mm is a portrait lens. Portraits require subjects. On a solo Easter road trip through western Norway in 5°C drizzle, there were almost no people on the streets — and the few who were weren’t the kind of visually striking subjects (cosplayers, street performers) that justify pulling out an 85mm prime.

Lesson learned:

  • The 85mm excels when there are interesting people to photograph: cosplayer events, street performers, bustling markets, travel companions.

  • Solo travel in off-season Nordic conditions offers none of these.

  • Carrying the Viltrox added 298g of unused weight to every outing.

  • Decision rule: Only pack the 85mm when you expect human subjects (group travel, events, high-season destinations with street life).

2.4 Sony HX90 — Never Really Used (But Tested)#

Verdict: Not needed for this trip. But the retirement test (see Section 3) changes the long-term calculus.

The HX90 sat in the bag throughout all actual photography. The focal length data proves this decisively: of 1,210 photos, only 6 (0.5%) were taken above 200mm equivalent — and those were APS-C crop shots at 250mm and 300mm on the Sigma, not HX90 shots. 300mm equivalent (Sigma at 200mm with APS-C crop) was sufficient for every composition during the trip. At no point did the author think, “I wish I had 720mm right now” — the compelling subjects (architecture, lighthouse, coastline, harbour) were all within comfortable superzoom range.

However, a deliberate, controlled test was conducted to quantify the image quality difference between the two systems at extreme telephoto.

This is the critical finding. At 720mm equivalent, the HX90’s real optical zoom produces genuinely better resolution than digitally enlarging the Sigma’s 300mm crop. The HX90 is optically resolving detail that the Sigma+crop+enlarge pipeline simply cannot reconstruct — you’re comparing real sensor data against interpolated pixels.

The exception is low light: when ISO climbs above ~1600 on the HX90, its tiny sensor produces so much noise that the noise itself destroys the resolution advantage. In that scenario, the A7C II’s clean high-ISO performance wins even with the enlargement penalty.

3.3 Analysis: When to Bring Both Cameras#

The test results, combined with the real-world experience on this trip, lead to a nuanced answer:

The “300mm is enough” realisation:

During the entire Ålesund trip, there was not a single moment where the author genuinely regretted not having 720mm. The compelling photographs were all captured within the Sigma’s native range (20-200mm) or its APS-C crop range (up to 300mm equivalent). Even at Alnes Fyr, where distant ships and seabirds could have been subjects, the story was told effectively at 200mm.

This suggests that for city and coastal landscape photography, 300mm is a practical ceiling — beyond which you’re chasing details that don’t materially improve the photograph.

The bottom-line decision framework:

Do you expect subjects beyond 300mm that you MUST capture in detail?
├── No → Leave HX90 at home. Sigma APS-C crop handles 300mm.
└── Yes → Will the light be good (daylight, well-lit)?
    ├── Yes → Bring HX90. Its optical 720mm genuinely outresolves digital crop.
    └── No → Leave HX90. Its tiny sensor fails in poor light anyway.

💡 The psychological takeaway from this trip: “When I can record distant things at 300mm, I actually don’t really regret that I couldn’t take more detailed pictures at 720mm.” This was a genuine, field-validated observation — not a theoretical compromise. For most travel photography, the story lives within 300mm. Beyond that, you’re chasing technical capability, not better photographs.

💡 When HX90 still justifies its existence: Despite the “300mm is enough” finding for this type of trip, the HX90 remains valuable as a purpose-specific tool for nature/wildlife outings where extreme telephoto is genuinely needed AND daylight is available. It also outperforms any smartphone at telephoto — so when you don’t bring the A7C II at all, the HX90 in a jacket pocket still delivers better telephoto images than the Samsung S25’s 69mm 3× zoom.

In practice, these presets struggled on the Ålesund trip for four reasons:

4.1 Why the Existing Presets Struggled#

Problem 1: City and nature are inter-weaved in Norway.

Ålesund is not Oslo or Tokyo. Norwegian towns are small, surrounded by mountains, fjords, and ocean. A single composition from Aksla includes the town centre, harbour, surrounding islands, distant snow-capped peaks, and sky — it’s simultaneously “city” and “landscape.” Switching between Preset 2 (“City Vivid”) and Preset 3 (“Landscape Bold”) for the same viewpoint makes no sense.

Problem 2: Volatile weather defeats subject-based classification.

Within a 30-minute walk through Ålesund, the light changed from flat overcast to drizzle to a brief burst of sun and back. The subject didn’t change (same street, same buildings), but the light changed dramatically. A subject-based preset stays the same; the light-based need changes constantly.

Problem 3: Low contrast and desaturated scenes.

Norwegian spring under heavy overcast produces images with remarkably little tonal separation and muted colours. Preset 2 (“City Vivid” — IN-based, Contrast +1, Saturation +1) wasn’t punchy enough to compensate. Preset 3 (“Landscape Bold” — Contrast +2, Saturation +2) was closer, but its aggressive Clarity +3 made overcast skies look unnaturally gritty without actually improving the scene.

Problem 4: Cold colour cast even in sunlight.

Even during the brief sunny moments, the light had a cool blue quality quite different from Mediterranean or Asian sunlight. The existing presets assume “sunny = warm” — but Nordic sun in April, low on the horizon and filtered through marine atmosphere, carries a distinctly cool bias. Neither Preset 2 nor 3 accounted for this.

4.2 A New Five-Preset Scheme: Lighting-Condition Based#

Instead of classifying by what you’re photographing (city vs landscape vs portrait), classify by how the light behaves. This approach works better in environments where subjects are mixed but lighting conditions are the primary variable.

In Norway (and Northern Europe generally), whether it’s sunny or overcast changes everything about the image — contrast, saturation, colour temperature, dynamic range. The subject (city, landscape, coast) matters far less to the colour rendering than the quality of the light falling on it.

💡 Why FL and not IN for overcast? IN amplifies what’s already there. ⚠️ Handling the sun-break transition: Nordic weather can switch from overcast to sunny in minutes. When the sun breaks through, the FL-based overcast preset will look noticeably warm — fine for a quick shot, but if the sun persists for more than a few minutes, switch to Preset 2 (Outdoor Sunny / IN-based) for cleaner, punchier results. On the A7C II mode dial (M2 = Sunny, M3 = Overcast), this is a one-click switch.

5. Practical Tips for Nordic Road Trip Photography#

A few field-learned observations specific to photographing in Norway during spring:

5.1 Weather-Adaptive Shooting Strategy#

  • Check yr.no obsessively. Norway’s weather service (yr.no) provides hyper-local, hour-by-hour forecasts with remarkable accuracy. On this trip, it correctly predicted a 15-minute sun window at Aksla — time enough to be in position and ready.

  • Shoot through the grey. Don’t wait for sun. Most of the trip’s best images were under overcast skies: the even light flatters architecture, eliminates harsh shadows, and produces moody, atmospheric landscapes. The grey is the light — use it.

  • Be ready for sun breaks. When the sun appears, you have minutes, not hours. Keep the camera accessible (Peak Design Capture Clip), not buried in a backpack. The Sigma 20-200mm’s single-lens convenience is invaluable here — no time for lens swaps when the light is fleeting.

  • Rain is not a stop signal. Light drizzle produces beautiful saturated colours on wet stone, roads, and foliage. The A7C II is weather-resistant. Wipe the front element periodically and keep shooting.
